Example of English-to-SQL translation
User Input: “Which region has the most customers?”
↓
SELECT r.name, COUNT(*) AS customer_count
FROM customers c
JOIN regions r ON c.region_id = r.id
GROUP BY r.name
ORDER BY customer_count DESC
LIMIT 1;
↓
Retrieved Answer:
Northeast (3 customers)
↓
System Output:
“The Northeast region has the most customers, totaling 3.”

Running a business is hard. In retail, you might find yourself with 2 hours to restock today's most popular item before customers find empty shelves. Your mind is already balancing the staffing schedule for tonight and the recent arrival of that large overseas delivery—and as soon as you switch focus, the house of cards will collapse, and you're spending extra time rebuilding the lost details.

Imagine if AI could help you mentally pick up right a task right where you left it. I built SQLHarness to help professionals automate the data retrieval part of that problem. If Claude Code can put together a working search bar for your website in less than 30 minutes, AI is clearly advanced enough to answer a simple question about your store data, right?

The purpose of this brief is to demonstrate the intuition behind SQLHarness’ proposed architecture.

How the semantic model was developed

  • Abstract Meaning Representation (AMR) looks past word order and keeps the underlying event: what happened, who or what caused it, and who or what it affected.
    • “A manager approved the budget” becomes three connected ideas: approve is the event, manager is the doer, and budget is the thing approved.
    • “The budget was approved by a manager” produces the same map because the meaning is the same.
  • SQLHarness applies the same idea to database questions. Its intent graph captures the data, calculation, rules, and requested answer in one stable map that can be checked against the database and translated into SQL.

How SQL fragments are selected

  • The intent graph becomes a parts list for the SQL query. Each labeled connection between two nodes describes one job, such as count customers, group them by region, or return a region name.
  • For each connection, SQLHarness searches a catalog of ready-made SQL pieces. It scores the candidates and keeps the piece that best fits both nodes, their relationship, and the database.
  • The selected pieces are joined into one query. If the catalog lacks a needed piece, the query cannot be completed—so the catalog must cover the SQL patterns the system may need.

What the study found

In the final five-query comparison, direct generation answered four correctly; both structured variants answered three.

The extra structure made failures easier to locate and execution easier to govern, but it did not improve the accuracy–cost tradeoff in this small study.
